If you've changed jobs a few times, there's a good chance you have a 401(k) or two sitting with a former employer. Old accounts are easy to forget. The statements go to an old email address, the login gets lost and life moves on.

That money still matters. It's part of your retirement picture, and part of what God has entrusted to you. When you leave a job, you generally have four options for an old 401(k). Here's how each one works and the tradeoffs people commonly weigh.

## 1. Leave it where it is

Many plans let you keep your savings in your former employer's plan after you leave. Some have rules for smaller balances, so check with the plan administrator.

**What people like about it.** There's nothing to do right now. Your investments stay as they are, and some employer plans offer low-cost investment options.

**What to weigh.** You can no longer contribute to the account, and you're limited to the plan's investment menu. Every old account is also one more login, one more set of statements and one more address to keep up to date. Over time, scattered accounts make it harder to see your full picture.

## 2. Roll it into your new employer's plan

If your new employer's plan accepts rollovers from other plans (not all do), you can move your old balance into it.

**What people like about it.** Fewer accounts to track. Your old savings and your new contributions sit in one place, under one login, and the money stays inside a workplace plan.

**What to weigh.** You're limited to the new plan's investment choices, and fees vary from plan to plan, so it helps to compare the two. You'll also have paperwork with both the old plan and the new one. Ask both plans about a direct rollover, where the money moves straight from one plan to the other. A check made out to you can come with tax withholding and a deadline to deposit the money in the new account.

## 3. Roll it into an IRA

An individual retirement account (IRA) is an account you open on your own, outside any employer. You can roll an old 401(k) into one, and many people use a single IRA to combine several old plans.

**What people like about it.** An IRA usually offers a much wider range of investment choices than an employer plan. Bringing several old accounts together can make your picture simpler.

**What to weigh.** Fees depend on the provider and the investments you choose, and they can be higher or lower than what you pay today. Rules on things like loans and protection from creditors can differ between workplace plans and IRAs. If your 401(k) holds both pre-tax and Roth money, each part has its own destination, so confirm how the rollover will be handled before you sign anything.

One more fair question. If someone suggests a rollover, ask how they're paid and whether the move changes that. A good advisor will be glad you asked.

## 4. Cash it out

You can also take the balance as cash.

**What people like about it.** The money is available right away.

**What to weigh.** This is the option with the biggest tax consequences. The withdrawal is generally taxed as income, and depending on your age, an early withdrawal penalty may apply on top of that. The plan will usually withhold some tax up front, so the check can be noticeably smaller than your balance. Money that leaves a retirement account also loses the tax advantages it had there. Some exceptions to the penalty exist, which is one more reason to talk with a tax professional before you decide.

## What people commonly weigh

Whichever way you're leaning, these are the questions that tend to matter most:

- **Fees.** What does each option cost each year, including fund expenses and account fees?
- **Investment choices.** Does the menu fit the way you want to invest?
- **Simplicity.** How many accounts, logins and statements do you want to keep track of?
- **Taxes and penalties.** Will this move create a tax bill? Could a penalty apply?
- **Paperwork.** What forms does each plan need, and who will follow up to make sure the money lands where it should?
- **Features and protections.** Do loans, withdrawal rules or creditor protections matter in your situation?

There's no single right answer for everyone. The right fit depends on your age, your other accounts, your tax picture and what you want your money to do.
